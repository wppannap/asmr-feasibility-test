At 3 a.m., more than 200 people were still watching a sleep-aid ASMR video on Bilibili. Bullet comments kept appearing: some viewers talked about upcoming exams, others said they had to go to work in a few hours. Some admitted they were exhausted but still wanted to watch one more video.

“The image of these awake people finding companionship in one another’s ‘sleeplessness’ really moved me,”

said Dr. Lucia Lu Chen, a guest lecturer at the School of Communication at Hong Kong Baptist University.

Her fieldwork on Bilibili inspired her research on ASMR.

The viewers were alone in their rooms, yet could see one another through the comments. Lucia found something ironic in the scene: everyone had opened a video meant to help them sleep, but together they were staying awake.

Why does the quiet after the lights go out make it harder for some young people to fall asleep?

The 2026 China Sleep Health Research White Paper reports that 48.5% of adults experience sleep anxiety, while 21% of university students do not fall asleep until after 2 a.m. Late nights and sleep anxiety are challenges for many young people.

Nina Zheng, a PhD student in psychology at the Education University of Hong Kong, is currently conducting sleep intervention research with her supervisor, Professor Esther Lau. She cautions that staying up late does not always mean someone has insomnia.

Some young people’s sleep schedules may simply be delayed, they go to bed and wake up later than most people. Others may experience heightened cognitive and emotional arousal before sleep. School, work and social media fill their attention during the day. At night, when external stimulation fades, the day’s pressures and worries can come back to mind. This repeated thinking is known as rumination.

“People may not be afraid of silence itself,”

Nina said.

Without outside sounds to draw their attention away, thoughts can take over. Whispers, tapping or other sounds can sometimes shift attention outward and help a person relax, though their effects vary from person to person.

That late-night search for comforting background sound is visible far beyond a single livestream. Global search data show that interest in ASMR has grown steadily across much of the world in recent years.

Act I

I | A refuge in the headphones: What are we listening to?

Some videos have no speech. Creators use brushes, fingernails, paper or other objects to make sounds. Others feature whispers, role-play and simulated personal care. The first type is often called “No Talking”; the second relies more on speech and a sense of companionship, and is often labelled “Whisper.”

The duration data show a difference between sleep videos and other videos. Longer videos can provide a continuous soundscape in the background. But a long video does not mean viewers listen to the end; some may fall asleep while it is still playing.

The two types of videos hold viewers’ attention in different ways. No Talking videos rely mainly on changing textures, the distance of the sound and the recording space. Videos with speech can use characters, conversation and pacing to create the feeling that someone is looking after the listener.

Video Duration: Sleep-Aid vs. Regular ASMR

Mark Grimshaw-Aagaard, a professor of acoustics at The Hong Kong University of Science and Technology (Guangzhou), said that one feature of No Talking videos is their “neutrality”. They do not carry a speaker’s gender, accent, intonation or cultural background. But neutrality does not necessarily mean greater immersion. Listeners’ experiences vary.

Anatomy of a Sleep-Aid Soundscape

In an analysis of videos with more than 10 million views each, the project found a recurring pattern. Early sections often feature triggers such as Picking, Scratching and Ear Cleaning, role-play or faster changes in sound. Later on, the sounds may shift to softer textures, more continuous rustling, repeated words, quiet counting or slower whispers.

In many sleep-aid ASMR videos, creators use sounds to catch the listener’s attention at first, then gradually slow the pace.

One No Talking video with about 2.4 million views begins with random, granular picking sounds. Near the end, it features the rustling of a Snow Bag and Fluffy Plants. Another whisper video, with about 2.13 million views, has viewership peaks during a conversation about retro digital gadgets and a slow farewell near the end. These examples suggest that listeners may not want the same kind of stimulation throughout a video.

Some videos with speech do not require listeners to follow every word. Repeated sounds such as “dididada” or quiet counting create the presence of a human voice without giving the listener much to understand. It can be thought of as “verbal white noise”: you hear someone speaking, but you do not have to follow the conversation closely.

Some videos also use more striking visual and audio effects such as a lash curler brought close to the camera, a brush tapping against a face, or hand movements layered with mouth sounds. These may create a little tension on screen while keeping the sounds within a controlled performance.

Act II

How Sound Physics and Camera Tricks Manufacture Intimacy on the Screen

Behind ASMR’s seemingly spontaneous late-night calm lies studio-grade acoustics, warm bedroom lighting and a familiar toolkit of props designed to soothe tired minds.

At 1am in a dark bedroom, a popular ASMR video feels disarmingly simple: a soft whisper, warm light and gentle hands brushing past the camera. Strip away the cosy glow, however, and what looks effortless turns out to be carefully engineered audio-visual craft.

Behind the lens, some top creators will even spend thousands on Neumann or Røde condenser microphones and silicone dummy heads moulded like human skulls. Unlike cheap dynamic mics that lose detail above 10 kilohertz, condenser capsules preserve delicate high-frequency textures that normally fade quickly through open air.

That physical detail is what fools the ear, says Prof. Mark Grimshaw-Aagaard, a sound perception specialist at HKUST. “The closer you are to a sound source, the higher the proportion of high-frequency sound reaches your ear,” he says. “That is how the brain knows someone is close.”

Creators also favour warm, amber lighting over harsh, cool tones. In our sample, 85% of the videos used soft warm hues, while only 15% featured cool blue or neon lights. That bedside-lamp glow reduces screen glare, helping tired eyes settle before sleep.

Beyond lighting, creators may branch into distinct styles and scenes. Roughly two-thirds look directly into the lens for whispered talking or skincare roleplay, while 30% go faceless. Across both talking and no-talking formats, familiar props recur: makeup brushes, bamboo earpicks, silicone ears, sponges and long nails.

Many listeners simply play ASMR in the background without looking at the screen, yet creators still work hard to build a cosy visual atmosphere. For Dr. Lucia Lu Chen, the guest lecturer at Hong Kong Baptist University’s Academy of Film, watching roleplay creates a gentle “parasocial atmosphere” that offers safe companionship.

However, watching at the screen can also break the spell. Seeing a brush scrape a microphone will pull listeners into analysing how the trick works instead of letting go.

“Watching the video actually diminishes auditory immersion,” Grimshaw-Aagaard says. “With your eyes closed and headphones on, you can truly let yourself go.”

III | Geography, gender & audience

Who Stands Behind the Microphone, and Who Listens in the Dark?

Across the global creator economy, two fault lines define modern ASMR: spoken companionship versus wordless soundscapes, and a shifting gender balance between creators and listeners.

Pull back from individual videos to the global platform, and two sharp divides shape how calm is produced and consumed. Across our 5,205 mapped YouTube channels, ASMR clusters heavily in Western and East Asian hubs, yet the two regions sound strikingly different.

Creator Concentration Across Major Markets

Cluster area = top 5% creators' video share · Hover to inspect

In our analysis, we compared 40 videos (20 Western and 20 East Asian). Among the Western videos, 80% used whispering or spoken roleplay, averaging about 35 minutes. By contrast, 70% of the East Asian videos had no talking, focusing on ear-cleaning and tapping that ran past 50 minutes and drew larger audiences.

For Grimshaw-Aagaard, silence helps videos cross borders. “A non-talking ASMR video is more neutral,” he says. “It doesn’t carry the gender of the speaker, their speech inflections, their accents, or their culture.” Chen cautions, however, that Chinese platforms also host countless eye-contact roleplay creators shaped by local algorithms.

Gender forms the second divide. Across 71 ASMR channels on FeedSpot’s 2026 top list (excluding 4 unrelated), women creators lead at 67.6%, while men account for 23.9%. According to a 2019 Empirical Musicology Review study of 30 popular ASMR videos, male creators made up just 6.7% of that earlier sample.

Fewer men reach the top list, and the top male channels hold a slightly higher median subscriber count than women. Women creators mainly focus on close-up whispering, skincare and nail tapping, while men lean toward soft-spoken narration, theatrical roleplay, gaming and history.

Listening to ASMR is not just a female habit. According to a 2025 study in Oxford’s Neuroscience of Consciousness, large-scale survey research finds no gender gap in how strongly men and women experience ASMR sensitivity.

Listening habits show the same balance. In a 2022 Deezer global survey of 12,136 listeners, men and women reported tuning in daily at almost identical rates, with 22% of women and 21% of men listening every day.

Where men and women differ is in what they want from ASMR. The same 2022 Deezer survey found that 45% of listeners prefer female voices and 30% have no preference, with women twice as flexible as men. Women mostly seek stress relief and sleep, while men more often look for focus, mood lifts or sensory pleasure.

Act IV

Relief, immunity or a gentle delay?

ASMR can be comforting, but it can also keep people watching. It is not easy to classify it simply as “helpful” or “harmful.”

Lucia connects the late-night scene to “revenge bedtime procrastination.” For some young people, the hours before sleep may be one of the few parts of the day that feel like their own. They know they need to sleep, but do not want to end that time just yet. ASMR can make continued viewing feel like self-care: “I’m not putting off sleep; I’m relaxing.”

Lucia observed two different habits. Some viewers return to familiar videos because they find them reassuring. Others say they have become “immune” to sounds that once worked for them, and start looking for new creators, stronger triggers or more elaborate performances. Lucia says the search for the right video can sometimes take a long time. People are trying to calm themselves while also looking for new stimulation.

Emily is careful about which ASMR videos she chooses before bed. Before playing one, she checks the comments to find out whether the sounds later in the video are ones she can tolerate, so they will not disturb her sleep.

“Once, I randomly picked an ASMR video. After listening for a while, I realized there was speech in it, and it woke me right up,” Emily said.

Nina says that if someone hears a sound they dislike or did not expect while falling asleep, it may wake them again or cause a brief micro-arousal.

She says ASMR comes in many forms and can be difficult to control in the same way as the fixed-frequency sounds used in experiments. “People have different preferences, so you can’t say that one kind of sound works for everyone,” she added.

Nina also noted that people who are used to falling asleep with audio may feel anxious when they are away from familiar sounds. That feeling is worth noticing, but it should not be treated as a clinical diagnosis.

The key is to notice whether ASMR helps you relax—or keeps stretching out the time before sleep.

Nina and Lucia offered a few suggestions for people experiencing sleep difficulties or using ASMR:

  1. 01

    Get more daylight during the day. Dim the lights and reduce screen use one to two hours before bed.

  2. 02

    If a sound keeps you alert or wakes you, switch to something steadier and familiar—or turn it off.

  3. 03

    Watch for caffeine in tea, chocolate and energy drinks. Keep naps to 20 minutes or less.

  4. 04

    Treat rest as valuable in itself, not just a way to be more productive the next day.

Nina says audio interventions are commonly used in sleep research in Hong Kong and overseas, and are considered a preferable option to medication.

In the future, researchers may also explore whether more varied and personalized sounds, including ASMR, can be used in sleep interventions, and how different sounds affect different people.
